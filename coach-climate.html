LED lighting load set: 100 watts per coach

The working allowance is 100 W of LED fixtures per coach, 400 W across all four coaches. The same allowance applies to the accessible coach. This covers normal interior, boarding and exit/orientation lighting; decorative Christmas lights, locomotive lamps and other show loads are separate.

For a proposed 24 V lighting circuit, that is 4.17 A per coach at the fixture bus. With an assumed 90% AC/DC efficiency and 0.9 input power factor, the rounded 448 W input allowance is about 2.1 A equivalent at 240 V for all four coaches. The actual feeder arrangement, converter and circuit protection remain to be selected; these numbers are not branch-circuit ratings.

The budget is supported by real vehicle-light examples: six Grote 61P71 lamps at 24 V × 0.46 A total 66.24 W, within the 70 W interior allowance. Final appearance, glare, dimming and exit-light performance still need matching.

Emergency illumination must remain available from its backup supply. Its lamps are counted within the lighting allowance; the emergency battery/charger and recharge load remain separate. Do not count the same lamp twice.

Yes, fresh-air ventilation is normal in enclosed passenger vehicles

Passenger-vehicle suppliers offer dedicated ventilation, and standard mini-splits primarily recirculate cabin air. Their indoor fan is not an outside-air supply. Retain a controlled fresh-air intake and an outlet/relief path for the enclosed coaches. Vehicle ventilation example ↗ · Mini-split ventilation limits ↗

The existing 250 W per-coach fan allowance stays provisional; it is not a selected motor rating. Select from the needed airflow at the actual filter and duct resistance, using the fullest permitted seating configuration, including fold-down seats. The fan moves air; the mini-split must also heat or cool that incoming air. Those are separate loads.

Does this bring hot air in while the mini-split runs? Yes, on a hot day. A controlled amount of outside air replaces stale cabin air; the mini-split removes the added heat and, when needed, moisture. The cooling calculation already includes an outside-air allowance, so it must not be added twice. A heat-recovery ventilator could reduce the temperature load, but adds equipment; no recovery credit is assumed here. Heat-recovery principle ↗ (building guidance, not vehicle approval). The intake must be located away from locomotive exhaust.

Current electrical boundary: replacing the old 120 W lighting input allowance with 112 W changes the four-coach auxiliary allowance from 4.320 to 4.288 kW. The remaining 250 W fan, 600 W brake-battery charger, 60 W emergency-light charger and 50 W controls per coach are still placeholders. The large charger allowances need a device-based review before a generator is chosen.

Brake compressor direction: develop an engine-driven compressor. Its main electric motor would come off the electrical schedule for that arrangement; its mechanical power, drive and electrical auxiliaries still need confirmation. Fresh air needs its own equipment and airflow check

The inherited 7.5 L/s per thermal person gives 247.5 L/s (524 cfm) per regular coach and 105 L/s (222 cfm) for the accessible coach. The extra 30 L/s per car in the load model is average door/leakage allowance; it is not a mechanical fresh-air supply. The rate still requires a vehicle-specific ventilation basis and maximum actual seating occupancy; the lower accessible-coach value is not accepted for a fuller fold-down-seat arrangement.

Mini-splits principally recirculate cabin air, so their fan airflow cannot be counted as this outside-air supply. Keep ventilation, filtration and distribution separate in the schedule. The next purchasable package starts with this worksheet

  1. Set the operating envelope: retain the proposed evening check points or include summer daytime duty; settle the indoor comfort and boarding recovery requirements.
  2. Specify the enclosure: insulation assemblies, actual safety glazing U/SHGC, window/door areas and seals. Reconcile the raised roof and accessible shell.
  3. Match standard units to delivered loads: obtain both sensible/latent cooling and low-temperature heating capacity, input power, defrost and starting data for each exact indoor/outdoor pairing. A nominal “ton” or BTU label alone is insufficient.
  4. Close ventilation and power together: add actual fresh-air fans and all coach loads, then size the power supply and confirm generator/drive compatibility.

Keep the installation simple: investigate independently controlled heat-pump systems for each coach. No between-coach refrigerant or hot-water system has been introduced. Actual unit count and mounting arrangement follow verified performance and the final coach body.
